# Quantum Computing in Cluck & Crow

This document is the deep dive: what's actually happening, physically and
mathematically, every time the Crow Hive "thinks." It's written for anyone
encountering quantum computing for the first time who'd like the real
concepts, not just an analogy. Each section maps directly to one function
or class in `quantum_ai.py`, and to one tutorial step you'll see in-game
(toggle Full Tutorial Mode with `T`).

.

## The big picture

Every Crow turn, the game needs to answer one question: *given the current
positions of every bird on the field, which single move should a Crow make
to push the flock toward the most stable arrangement?*

"Most stable" here has a precise meaning, borrowed from game theory: a
**Nash equilibrium** of the underlying congestion game, the arrangement
where no single bird could improve its own side's position by moving away
from it unilaterally. Finding that arrangement exactly means checking every
possible way to split the birds between the two coops — and the number of
possible splits doubles with every additional bird. Ten birds means 1,024
splits; twenty birds means over a million; thirty means over a billion.
This doubling, called **exponential scaling**, is precisely the kind of
wall that quantum algorithms are designed to push against (not eliminate —
push against) using superposition and interference, which is the actual
reason this game routes the decision through a quantum circuit instead of
just looping over every possibility classically.

The pipeline has six stages. Stages 1-2 are classical preprocessing
(encoding the problem so a quantum computer can use it). Stages 3-6 happen
on an actual quantum circuit, simulated locally via Qiskit's Aer backend.

```
Flock state
   │  STEP 1: encoding
   ▼
Boolean logic clauses (NAE-SAT)
   │  STEP 2: reduction
   ▼
Max-Cut graph
   │  STEP 3: superposition
   ▼
Quantum circuit, all qubits in equal superposition
   │  STEP 4: entanglement
   ▼
Qubits linked via RZZ gates along graph edges
   │  STEP 5: annealing / interference
   ▼
Distribution sharpened toward good splits
   │  STEP 6: measurement
   ▼
One classical bitstring (the Hive's answer)
   │  classical post-processing
   ▼
Single legal Crow move
```

---

## Step 1: Encoding the problem

**Function:** `state_to_nae_clauses()`

Quantum computers (like classical ones) don't understand "cows" or "hens"
or "coops." Before any of this can become a circuit, the real-world
situation has to become **boolean logic** — true/false statements about
variables.

For every feather-gene value (e.g. "gene A is Hen-colored"), the game
gathers every bird that currently expresses that exact value into a group.
Each group becomes what's called a **Not-All-Equal (NAE) clause**: a
constraint saying "these variables should not all end up assigned the same
boolean value." Practically, this encodes flock cohesion — birds sharing a
visible trait are linked, and the optimizer will try to keep them from
being split in a perfectly even, disorganized way.

This first move — turning a real situation into formal logic — is the same
first step you'd take for *any* real combinatorial optimization problem:
flight scheduling becomes constraints about which flights can't overlap;
chip layout becomes constraints about which wires can't cross; vaccine
distribution becomes constraints about which routes can't conflict. The
specific problem changes; the move of "write it as logic clauses over
boolean variables" doesn't.

## Step 2: Problem reduction

**Functions:** `nae_to_nae3()`, `nae3_to_graph()`

Computer scientists love proving that two problems which look totally
different are secretly the same problem wearing different clothes. This
step performs two such reductions back to back.

**NAE-SAT → NAE-3-SAT.** The clauses from Step 1 can have any number of
birds in them. But there's a well-studied special case — NAE-3-SAT — where
every clause has *exactly* three variables. Any larger clause can be
rewritten as a chain of 3-variable clauses by introducing temporary
**auxiliary variables** (the `AuxVar` class) that don't correspond to any
real bird; they only exist to make the chain of logic work out, the same
way you might introduce a helper variable when solving an algebra problem
by substitution.

**NAE-3-SAT → Max-Cut.** It turns out NAE-3-SAT is *exactly* equivalent to
a graph problem called **Max-Cut**: given a graph, split its nodes into two
groups so that the heaviest possible total weight of edges ends up
connecting the two groups ("cut" between them). The reduction works like
this:

- Every boolean variable becomes **two** graph nodes: one representing "this
  variable is true," one representing "this variable is false." These two
  nodes are joined by a deliberately very heavy edge, so that any good
  split is essentially forced to put them on opposite sides — a variable
  can't sensibly be both true and false at once.
- Every 3-variable clause becomes a small triangle of light edges between
  its three literal-nodes, because "not all three can be equal" turns out
  to be mathematically the same condition as "at least one edge of this
  triangle must be cut."

A huge number of real NP-hard problems — scheduling, circuit layout,
portfolio selection, vaccine routing — are secretly Max-Cut, or reduce to
it in a few steps like this one. It's one of the most heavily studied
"universal" combinatorial problems precisely because so many other things
collapse into it.

## Step 3: Superposition

**Function:** `build_annealing_circuit()` (the Hadamard layer)

Now we leave classical preprocessing behind. Every graph node from Step 2
becomes one **qubit** in a real quantum circuit. The first thing that
happens to each qubit is a **Hadamard gate** (`qc.h(q)` in the code).

Classically, a bit is either 0 or 1 — one value at a time, full stop. A
qubit can be placed into a **superposition**: a genuine combination of |0⟩
and |1⟩ simultaneously, not "we don't know which it is yet" but
"both, with the math to prove it." A Hadamard gate applied to a qubit
starting at |0⟩ produces an equal-weighted superposition of |0⟩ and |1⟩.

Here's the part that matters: apply that to *every* qubit in the circuit,
and the whole system enters a superposition of **all 2ⁿ possible
combinations at once**, where n is the number of qubits. With, say, 16
qubits (a typical graph size for a Medium game), the circuit is now
representing 65,536 different possible Max-Cut splits *simultaneously*,
in one quantum state. This is the seed of the speedup: instead of checking
splits one at a time, the circuit is, in a very real physical sense,
exploring every split in parallel.

## Step 4: Entanglement and phase

**Function:** `build_annealing_circuit()` (the RZZ gates)

Superposition alone doesn't solve anything — you need a way to make the
*good* splits more prominent than the bad ones. That requires
**entanglement**: gates that link two qubits together so that what happens
to one affects the other.

The circuit applies an **RZZ gate** between every pair of qubits that are
connected by an edge in the Max-Cut graph. RZZ doesn't just correlate the
two qubits' eventual measured values — it links their **relative quantum
phase**, a property of the wave-like math underlying the qubit's state
that doesn't show up directly when you measure a single qubit, but
critically affects how different parts of the superposition interfere with
each other later. The strength of the rotation applied by each RZZ gate is
scaled by that edge's weight in the graph — heavily-weighted edges (like
the "this variable can't be both true and false" consistency edges from
Step 2) get pushed harder toward ending up on opposite sides.

Entanglement is the property that has no classical analogue at all — there
is no equivalent "linked coin flip" in ordinary probability that behaves
the way entangled qubits do. It's also the resource that makes Step 5
possible: without entangled phases, there would be nothing for
interference to act on.

## Step 5: Quantum annealing and interference

**Function:** `build_annealing_circuit()` (the alternating RX/RZZ loop),
controlled by the `time_steps` parameter

This is where the Hive actually "thinks," in the sense of converging
toward a good answer rather than a random one. The circuit alternates,
over several rounds (`time_steps`), between two kinds of gates:

- **Mixing gates** (`qc.rx`): keep exploring broadly, similar in spirit to
  re-randomizing.
- **Cost gates** (the same `RZZ` gates from Step 4, now applied repeatedly):
  reinforce the actual Max-Cut objective.

The *ratio* between how strongly each type is applied shifts smoothly over
the rounds — early on, mixing dominates (explore broadly); by the final
round, the cost Hamiltonian dominates (commit to good answers). This
schedule is a simplified version of **quantum annealing** and is closely
related to the physical principle behind **adiabatic quantum computing**:
if you change a quantum system's "rules" slowly enough, it tends to stay
in (or near) its lowest-energy, most-stable configuration the whole way
through — which here corresponds to a good Max-Cut split.

The mechanism that actually sorts good splits from bad ones is
**interference**. Because every possible split exists simultaneously in
superposition (Step 3) with phases linked by entanglement (Step 4), the
contributions from different splits can reinforce each other
(**constructive interference**, amplifying the probability of measuring
that outcome) or cancel each other out (**destructive interference**,
suppressing it). Good Max-Cut splits, shaped by the cost gates, tend to
constructively interfere with each other; bad splits tend to destructively
cancel. This is the same fundamental phenomenon — interference between
superposed quantum amplitudes — that powers **Grover's search algorithm**,
just applied here to an annealing schedule instead of Grover's specific
"amplitude amplification" recipe.

**This is also the Hive's literal difficulty dial.** `time_steps` controls
how many rounds this schedule gets to run before measurement:

- **Few steps** (e.g. 2): the schedule barely leaves "explore broadly"
  before time runs out. The cost Hamiltonian never gets to dominate, so
  interference doesn't have enough rounds to meaningfully sharpen the
  distribution. The measured outcome stays close to a noisy coin-flip
  across many candidate splits — an "Erratic" Hive, more beatable.
- **Many steps** (e.g. 14+): the schedule has plenty of room to ramp the
  cost Hamiltonian's strength way up relative to mixing, giving
  constructive interference many rounds to reinforce the genuinely best
  splits. The resulting distribution sharpens hard around the optimum — a
  "Ruthless" Hive, much harder to outmaneuver.

You can watch this difference directly: raise or lower the Hive's
annealing steps mid-game with `+`/`-`, then look at the probability bar
chart after the next measurement. Low settings show several similarly-
sized bars (a flat, uncertain distribution); high settings show one
dominant bar towering over the rest (a sharp, confident distribution).

## Step 6: Measurement and collapse

**Functions:** `run_circuit()`, `best_bitstring()`, `top_results()`

Up to this point, nothing about the circuit's state is "real" in the
classical sense — it's a single quantum state representing many candidate
answers superposed together, with their relative likelihoods shaped by the
annealing schedule. **Measurement** is the step that forces this into one
concrete, classical answer: each qubit collapses to a definite 0 or 1, and
you get one bitstring.

Crucially, measurement is probabilistic, not deterministic. A good
annealing schedule makes the best splits *much more likely* to be the
measured outcome — but never the *only possible* outcome (short of a
perfect, idealized, infinitely long schedule). This is why the game runs
the circuit 2000 times (`shots=2000`) rather than once: with enough
repeated measurements, you build up a real statistical picture — a
**probability distribution** over outcomes — instead of trusting a single
roll of the dice. `best_bitstring()` picks the single most-frequent
outcome as the Hive's working answer, but `top_results()` keeps the next
several runner-ups too, specifically so the in-game probability chart can
show you that distribution honestly: "this was the most likely outcome, at
roughly this percentage, with these other candidates not far behind" — not
"this is THE answer, full stop."

This distinction — *likely* versus *certain* — is arguably the single most
important intuition to walk away with about how real quantum computers
work. They are not magic deterministic oracles. They reshape probability
distributions so that good answers become likely, then you sample from
that distribution (possibly many times) and act on what comes up most.

### A practical, unglamorous detail: transpilation

Before the circuit actually runs, `run_circuit()` passes it through
Qiskit's **transpiler** with `optimization_level=3`. Transpilation rewrites
a circuit into an equivalent one that's cheaper to execute — fusing gates
together, removing redundant operations, and (on real hardware)
rearranging things to respect a chip's limited qubit connectivity. It
doesn't change what the circuit computes, only how efficiently it gets
there. As the flock grows (more birds → more NAE-3-SAT clauses → more
graph edges → more RZZ gates), an unoptimized circuit's depth grows fast;
aggressive transpilation is what keeps the simulator snappy as games scale
up to Hard difficulty.

## After measurement: turning an answer into a move

**Functions:** `target_side_for_bird()`, `pick_best_move()` (also see the
alternative implementation in `cost_function.py`)

The measured bitstring tells the Hive its ideal arrangement — which side
of the Max-Cut split every bird-variable landed on. But the Hive can only
make one *legal* move per turn: flip a single Crow-species bird's coop
side. The final step is purely classical bookkeeping: check every Crow
bird the Hive is allowed to move, see whether its current side matches or
disagrees with the quantum-annealed target, and pick whichever single move
resolves the biggest disagreement. This is the bridge from "the math says
this configuration would be ideal" to "therefore, move this specific
bird" — the quantum circuit decided *what's good*; this last step decides
*what to actually do about it*, within the game's rules.

## Why use quantum at all, here?

Worth being honest about: at the bird-counts this game actually reaches (a
few dozen qubits at most), a classical laptop can brute-force the *exact*
best Max-Cut split in well under a second — that's exactly what
`brute_force_best_cut()` in `quantum_ai.py` does, included specifically so
you can compare it against the quantum circuit's answer if you're curious.
The quantum approach isn't faster *at this scale*; classical brute force
hasn't hit the exponential wall hard enough yet to need rescuing.

What this game demonstrates instead is the *technique*, end to end, using
exactly the same conceptual pipeline that real quantum-optimization
research uses on much larger problems where the exponential wall is a
genuine obstacle: encode as logic, reduce to Max-Cut, superpose, entangle,
anneal, measure, sample. Every piece here is a real, legitimate quantum
operation running on a real (simulated) quantum circuit via Qiskit — just
deliberately kept small enough that you can watch every step happen, and
small enough to sanity-check against a brute-force answer when you want
to see how close the quantum result actually got.

## Glossary

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| **Qubit** | The quantum analogue of a bit; can be placed in superposition rather than only 0 or 1. |
| **Superposition** | A genuine combination of multiple states at once, not just uncertainty about which one it "really" is. |
| **Entanglement** | A link between qubits where their combined state can't be described by treating them separately — what happens to one is tied to the other. |
| **Phase** | A property of a quantum state's wave-like description that affects how it interferes with other states, even though it isn't directly visible in a single measurement. |
| **Interference** | Combining amplitudes from different parts of a superposition so they reinforce (constructive) or cancel (destructive) each other. |
| **Quantum annealing** | Gradually shifting a quantum circuit's "rules" from broad exploration toward a specific objective, so the final state is likely to represent a good (ideally optimal) solution. |
| **Measurement / collapse** | Forcing a quantum superposition into one definite classical outcome; the chance of any specific outcome depends on how the superposition was shaped beforehand. |
| **Max-Cut** | A graph problem: split nodes into two groups maximizing the total weight of edges between the groups. |
| **NAE-SAT / NAE-3-SAT** | Boolean satisfiability variants where the constraint is "not all of these variables are equal," restricted to 3-variable clauses in the "-3" version. |
| **Transpilation** | Rewriting a quantum circuit into an equivalent but more efficient one before running it. |
| **Shots** | The number of times a quantum circuit is run and measured, to build up a statistical picture of its output distribution. |
